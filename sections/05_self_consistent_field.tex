\section{Self-consistent field procedure}

After expansion in a finite basis, the one-electron Kohn--Sham equations become a matrix eigenvalue problem. For a general, non-orthogonal basis this problem is

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H}\mat{C} = \mat{S}\mat{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
\end{equation}

where the columns of \(\mat{C}\) contain the basis-set coefficients of the Kohn--Sham orbitals, \(\mat{S}\) is the overlap matrix, and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) is the diagonal matrix of orbital energies. Plane waves are orthonormal, so \(\mat{S}\) is the identity matrix and the problem simplifies to

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H}\mat{C} = \mat{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}.
    \label{eq:roothaan}
\end{equation}

For a single orbital, \cref{eq:roothaan} reads \(\mat{H}\vec{c}_{i}=\varepsilon_i\vec{c}_{i}\). If \(N_{\text{pw}}\) plane waves are used, explicitly storing the complete Hamiltonian requires memory proportional to \(N_{\text{pw}}^{2}\), while a full diagonalization requires approximately \(N_{\text{pw}}^{3}\) operations. Both costs rapidly become prohibitive.

For clarity, we consider a spin-unpolarized, closed-shell system at zero electronic temperature, so that every occupied spatial orbital contains two electrons. The electron density is then

\begin{equation}
    \rho(\vec{r}) = 2\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{elec}}/2} |\psi_{i}(\vec{r})|^{2}.
    \label{eq:edens_analytical}
\end{equation}

The density and the ground-state total energy in \cref{eq:gth-total-energy} therefore require only the \(N_{\text{elec}}/2\) occupied orbitals, rather than all \(N_{\text{pw}}\) eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian. In practice, a calculation may request some additional states: metals and finite-temperature calculations can have partially occupied bands, while response properties, excited-state methods and band-gap calculations also require unoccupied states. Here we restrict attention to the occupied states needed for a zero-temperature ground-state calculation.

Iterative eigensolvers exploit this difference in size and calculate only a selected part of the spectrum. The plane-wave Hamiltonian is Hermitian and highly structured, but it is not generally sparse when represented as a matrix in reciprocal space: the kinetic term is diagonal, a local potential produces a convolution, and the non-local pseudopotential adds projector terms. The useful property is that the action \(\vec{c}\mapsto\mat{H}\vec{c}\) can be evaluated efficiently without ever constructing the full matrix. Such an implementation is called \textbf{matrix-free}. Arnoldi, Lanczos and Davidson methods are examples of iterative eigensolvers that can use this matrix-free operation.\cite{1951:arnoldi,1975:davidson} We next discuss Arnoldi because its construction illustrates the central idea and because an implicitly restarted implementation is available through SciPy's ARPACK interface.\cite{arnoldi_scipy}

\subsection{Arnoldi method}

The central idea of Arnoldi is to replace one very large eigenvalue problem by a much smaller one. The method constructs an adaptive basis of trial orbitals and represents the Hamiltonian in that trial space. Diagonalizing the resulting small matrix determines how the trial orbitals should be mixed to approximate the desired Kohn--Sham orbitals.

During one Arnoldi solve, the electron density and hence the Hamiltonian are held fixed. Starting from a normalized vector \(\vec{q}_{1}\), the method explores the \(m\)-dimensional \textbf{Krylov subspace}

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K}_{m}(\mat{H},\vec{q}_{1}) =
    \operatorname{span}\left\{
    \vec{q}_{1},\mat{H}\vec{q}_{1},\mat{H}^{2}\vec{q}_{1},\ldots,
    \mat{H}^{m-1}\vec{q}_{1}\right\}.
    \label{eq:krylov-space}
\end{equation}

The vectors in \cref{eq:krylov-space} should not be used as written because repeated multiplication makes them nearly linearly dependent. Arnoldi instead constructs orthonormal vectors \(\{\vec{q}_{1},\ldots,\vec{q}_{m}\}\) spanning the same space. At iteration \(j\), it first applies the Hamiltonian,

\begin{equation}
    \vec{w}=\mat{H}\vec{q}_{j},
\end{equation}

and then removes all components that point along trial orbitals already present,

\begin{align}
    h_{ij} &= \vec{q}_{i}^{\dagger}\vec{w}, & i&=1,\ldots,j,\\
    \vec{w} &\leftarrow \vec{w}-h_{ij}\vec{q}_{i}, & i&=1,\ldots,j.
    \label{eq:arnoldi-orthogonalize}
\end{align}

Here \(\dagger\) denotes the complex-conjugate transpose. The remaining component is normalized to produce the next basis vector,

\begin{align}
    h_{j+1,j} &= \|\vec{w}\|,\\
    \vec{q}_{j+1} &= \frac{\vec{w}}{h_{j+1,j}}.
    \label{eq:arnoldi-normalize}
\end{align}

Chemically, each \(\vec{q}_{j}\) is a trial orbital expressed in the original plane-wave basis. Application of \(\mat{H}\) reveals new directions relevant to the energy spectrum, while the orthogonalization in \cref{eq:arnoldi-orthogonalize} prevents the method from repeatedly rediscovering the same direction.

Collecting the trial orbitals in \(\mat{Q}_{m}=[\vec{q}_{1},\ldots,\vec{q}_{m}]\) produces the Arnoldi relation

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H}\mat{Q}_{m}
    = \mat{Q}_{m}\mat{H}_{m}
    +h_{m+1,m}\vec{q}_{m+1}\vec{e}_{m}^{\,T},
    \qquad
    \mat{H}_{m}=\mat{Q}_{m}^{\dagger}\mat{H}\mat{Q}_{m}.
    \label{eq:arnoldi-relation}
\end{equation}

The projected matrix \(\mat{H}_{m}\) is only \(m\times m\) and has upper-Hessenberg form, meaning that all entries below its first subdiagonal are zero. It can therefore be diagonalized inexpensively,

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H}_{m}\vec{y}_{i}=\theta_{i}\vec{y}_{i}.
    \label{eq:ritz-small}
\end{equation}

The eigenvalue \(\theta_i\) is called a \textbf{Ritz value}. The corresponding \textbf{Ritz vector} in the original plane-wave basis is

\begin{equation}
    \vec{u}_{i}=\mat{Q}_{m}\vec{y}_{i}.
    \label{eq:ritz-vector}
\end{equation}

Thus, \(\vec{y}_{i}\) gives the coefficients with which the trial orbitals must be mixed to form an approximate Kohn--Sham orbital \(\vec{u}_{i}\). Its accuracy is measured by the eigenproblem residual

\begin{equation}
    \vec{r}_{i}=\mat{H}\vec{u}_{i}-\theta_{i}\vec{u}_{i},
    \qquad
    \|\vec{r}_{i}\|
    =\left|h_{m+1,m}\vec{e}_{m}^{\,T}\vec{y}_{i}\right|.
    \label{eq:ritz-residual}
\end{equation}

An eigenpair is accepted when its residual norm is smaller than a chosen tolerance. Monitoring the residual is more reliable than checking only whether the orbital energy has stopped changing. For degenerate states, individual orbitals are not unique because any orthonormal mixture within the degenerate space is equally valid; in that case it is the subspace spanned by those orbitals that should converge.

The requested number of eigenpairs cannot be increased without cost. More states generally require a larger Krylov space, more stored vectors and more orthogonalization work. Because allowing the space to grow indefinitely would eventually become expensive, practical implementations periodically \textbf{restart}: they retain the most useful Ritz information and build a new, compact Krylov space. SciPy's \texttt{eigs} routine uses the implicitly restarted Arnoldi method.\cite{arnoldi_scipy}

The Kohn--Sham Hamiltonian is Hermitian. For a Hermitian matrix, Arnoldi reduces in exact arithmetic to the closely related \textbf{Lanczos method}, and \(\mat{H}_{m}\) becomes tridiagonal. SciPy's \texttt{eigsh} routine uses an implicitly restarted Lanczos method for a real-symmetric problem; for a complex-Hermitian input, SciPy currently delegates to \texttt{eigs}.\cite{lanczos_scipy} The general Arnoldi construction is presented here because it also applies when the implementation treats the Hamiltonian as a general complex operator.

Finally, ``lowest'' must not be confused with ``smallest in magnitude.'' In a chemical calculation the occupied states have the smallest \emph{algebraic} eigenvalues, meaning the energies at the lower end of the spectrum, whereas an eigenvalue of smallest magnitude is merely closest to zero. In SciPy this distinction corresponds, for a Hermitian problem, to \texttt{which='SA'} versus \texttt{which='SM'}. Arnoldi-type methods often converge more readily to large-magnitude eigenvalues; transformations such as shift-invert can improve access to other parts of the spectrum but require the solution of linear systems.\cite{lanczos_scipy}

Only Hamiltonian--vector products are needed throughout the Krylov construction. For the plane-wave Hamiltonian, the kinetic contribution is multiplied in reciprocal space, all multiplicative local potentials---ionic, Hartree and exchange-correlation---are applied in real space using FFTs, and the non-local projector contribution is added in reciprocal space. This matrix-free operation was detailed in \cref{eq:gth-hamiltonian} and the accompanying pseudocode.

In compact form, Arnoldi repeatedly applies \(\mat{H}\), orthogonalizes the new trial orbital, diagonalizes the small projected Hamiltonian, and tests the Ritz residuals. It expands or restarts the trial space until all requested eigenpairs satisfy the tolerance.

\subsection{Electron density mixing}

The Hartree and exchange-correlation potentials depend on the electron density. To display this dependence explicitly, \cref{eq:roothaan} can be written as

\begin{equation}
    \mat{H}[\rho]\mat{C} = \mat{C}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
    \label{eq:roothaan_explicit}
\end{equation}

where the coefficient matrix \(\mat{C}\) contains the plane-wave coefficients of the Kohn--Sham orbitals,

\begin{equation}
    C_{ij} = \tilde{\psi}_{j}(\vec{G}_{i}),
\end{equation}

and the occupied columns determine the new real-space density,

\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\text{out}}(\vec{r}) = 2\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{elec}} / 2} \left|\ifftpw \left[ \tilde{\psi}_{j}(\vec{G}) \right] \right|^{2}.
    \label{eq:edens}
\end{equation}

Equations \eqref{eq:roothaan_explicit} and \eqref{eq:edens} form a feedback loop: the input density determines the Hamiltonian, and solving that Hamiltonian produces an output density. A self-consistent solution satisfies \(\rho_{\text{out}}=\rho_{\text{in}}\). The SCF procedure therefore starts from an initial density, constructs \(\mat{H}[\rho_{\text{in}}]\), solves its fixed-Hamiltonian eigenproblem, constructs \(\rho_{\text{out}}\), and repeats until the density residual and total energy in \cref{eq:gth-total-energy} are sufficiently converged. A schematic overview is shown in \cref{fig:scf}.

        \input{img/fig10_scf}
    }
    \captionof{figure}{Self-consistent field procedure within plane-wave density functional theory.}
    \label{fig:scf}
\end{Figure}

The two nested iterations have different purposes. During the inner Arnoldi or Lanczos solve, \(\rho_{\text{in}}\) and \(\mat{H}[\rho_{\text{in}}]\) remain fixed, and convergence is assessed using the eigenpair residuals in \cref{eq:ritz-residual}. Only after the requested eigenpairs have converged is \(\rho_{\text{out}}\) constructed and the outer SCF iteration advanced. Orbitals from the preceding SCF step can provide useful starting information because consecutive Hamiltonians are often similar.

Simply replacing \(\rho_{\text{in}}\) by \(\rho_{\text{out}}\) may cause oscillation or ``overshooting.'' The simplest stabilization is linear density mixing,

\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\text{in}}^{(n+1)} = \alpha\rho_{\text{out}}^{(n)}
    +(1-\alpha)\rho_{\text{in}}^{(n)},
    \qquad 0<\alpha\leq 1,
    \label{eq:linear-mixing}
\end{equation}

where \(\alpha\) is the mixing parameter. A smaller value usually improves stability but may slow convergence. More advanced schemes use the history of previous density residuals to propose a better update.

\subsection{Solutions and visualization}

At self-consistency, the occupied solutions of \cref{eq:roothaan} are the Kohn--Sham states that minimize the energy functional subject to the requirement that the states remain orthonormal. For molecular systems, these states are commonly called molecular orbitals. Their plane-wave coefficients are stored in the columns of \(\mat{C}\). Visualization of these orbitals is fairly straightforward via application of \cref{eq:fft_pw_inverse}, yet there are two important things to note.

Since $\psi(\vec{r}): \mathbb{R}^{3} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$, typical construction of contour plots or isosurfaces requires evaluation of both real and imaginary parts of $\psi(\vec{r})$. Furthermore, especially for isosurfaces, a relatively fine grid is needed. The sampling grid generated by \cref{eq:fft_pw_inverse} depends on the size of the basis set. As the size is typically kept to a minimum for the sake of computational efficiency, the sampling grid is relatively coarse.

To avoid having to sample both the real and imaginary part of the wave function, we can apply certain transformations to the wave function. These transformations should however keep the Kohn-Sham states orthonormal with respect to each other and should not affect the total electron density nor the kinetic energy of the states. Multiplying a wave function by a phase factor $\exp(i\varphi)$ will not change any of these properties.\footnote{Fundamentally speaking, one is also allowed to mix the occupied molecular orbitals among each other. This allows one to localize the molecular orbitals. We will not discuss such aspects here.} Thus, we can generate an equally valid new state from an existing one by a phase angle $\varphi \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ such that

\begin{equation}
    \psi\prime(\vec{r}) \leftarrow \psi(\vec{r}) \cdot \exp \left(i \varphi \right).
    \label{eq:psi_transform}
\end{equation}

Observe that the operation in \cref{eq:psi_transform} always keeps the magnitude of the complex number conserved. Thus, its application will not affect the electron density corresponding to $\psi(\vec{r}_{i})$ and allows us to morph the complex scalar field to a real one. For our use case, we wish to find the optimal $\varphi$ such that the sum of squares of the real part of the molecular orbital is maximized, i.e.

\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\text{real}} = \max_\varphi \left(\sum_{i}\mathfrak{R}\left[ \psi(\vec{r}_{i}) \cdot \exp \left(i \varphi \right)\right]^{2} \right).
    \label{eq:optimize_real}
\end{equation}

This is a non-linear optimization problem and an efficient way to tackle this is to use the \texttt{differential\_evolution} method of Scipy.\cite{differential_evolution}

Another potential problem to address involved the suboptimal sampling size, for which upsampling techniques can be used. Perhaps the most elegant upsampling technique is one that makes use of the Fast Fourier Transform. Consider a Kohn-Sham state $\psi(\vec{r})_{[32]}$ as defined on a grid in real-space using 32 points per Cartesian direction. In reciprocal-space, the same wave function is represented by $\tilde{\psi}(\vec{G})_{[32]}$. To upsample the real-space grid, we first add to $\tilde{\psi}(\vec{G})_{[32]}$ additional plane wave vectors $\vec{G}\prime$, all with $\tilde{\psi}(\vec{G}\prime) = 0$. This process is sometimes called ``padding''. For this strategy to work, the new number of sampling points per Cartesian direction must be an integer multiple of the old number of sampling points. For example, we can choose to upsample the original molecular orbital by a factor of 4, yielding $\tilde{\psi}(\vec{G})_{[128]}$ in reciprocal-space.

Next, the coefficients $\tilde{\psi}(\vec{G})_{[128]}$ can be cast back to real-space using the inverse Fourier transform. Importantly, we have to be cautious about the fact that we have changed the number of sampling. \cref{eq:fft_pw_inverse} remains valid, but the constant $C_{t}$ has changed and we have to use adjust \cref{eq:ct} such that $N_{x},N_{y},N_{z}$ correspond to the new values. To put this in other words, if we have upsampled by a factor of 4, the constant $C_{t}$ decreases by a factor of $4^{3}=64$.

\subsubsection{Demonstration of phase-rotation for \ce{BH3}}


To demonstrate the principle of ``phase-rotation'' for visualization purposes, consider the \ce{BH3} molecule calculated in a $10 \times 10 \times 10$ \AA\; unit cell using 32 sampling points per Cartesian direction. The calculation is performed using the \texttt{PyPWDFT}\cite{pypwdft} program. In this demonstration, we will focus on the double-degenerate set of orbitals with $E$-symmetry, i.e. the third and fourth occupied molecular orbitals. In \cref{fig:bh3_before}, the electron density of $\psi_{3}$ and $\psi_{4}$ is sampled in the plane in which the \ce{BH3} molecule resides. For these settings, the calculation converges such that $\psi_{3}$ has a negligible imaginary component. Interestingly, the $\psi_{4}$ molecular orbital is smeared out over both real and imaginary domains. 

\begin{Figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{img/fig11.pdf}

    \label{fig:bh3_before}
\end{Figure}

We now proceed to apply \cref{eq:optimize_real} to the two molecular orbitals with the aim of optimizing the real component of these orbitals by multiplication of a phase-factor $\exp \left(i \varphi \right)$. In \cref{tab:phase-rotation}, the total electron density for $\psi_{3}$ and $\psi_{4}$, prior and after this ``phase-rotation'', is provided. From this Table, it is clear that $\psi_{3}$ already has - within numerical approximation - all its electron density in the real domain. In contrast, for $\psi_{4}$ only $\pm$73\% of its electron density resides in the real domain, the other $\pm$27\% in the imaginary domain.

This is further reflected in \cref{fig:bh3_after}, where it can be seen that for $\varphi = 31\textdegree$ the total electron density corresponding to the real part of the wave function equals 1.0.
